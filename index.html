Try this
  1. Your line starts flat at ȳ. Its SSR equals SST: this is predicting with the mean and ignoring x.
  2. Hide the OLS line and tilt yours to make SSR as small as you can. Then show OLS. You cannot beat it.
  3. Move the height away from ȳ. SSR grows by exactly n·d²: the best line always passes through (x̄, ȳ).
  4. Set σ = 0. Every point is on the line, r = ±1 and r² = 1.
  5. Set the true slope to 0. r and r² collapse towards 0.
reg y x predict yhat, xb gen residuals = y - yhat correlate y x

Fitting a line by least squares

Your line its residuals ûᵢ OLS line (x̄, ȳ)

SSR for every possible slope

SSR of lines through (x̄, ȳ) your line

OLS solves ∂SSR/∂β̂₀ = 0 and ∂SSR/∂β̂₁ = 0: the bottom of this parabola. Your SSR = SSR at your slope + n·d², where d is how far your line misses ȳ at x̄.

Try this

        
    
          
  1. Look at the faint lines: the same x's with fresh errors. Your one sample is a single draw of β̂₁.
    2. 
          
  2. Switch 95% to 80%. The interval gets narrower: less certainty, smaller net.
    3. 
          
  3. Run the repeated samples. The histogram matches the normal curve with SD σ / √SSTₓ.
    4. 
          
  4. Raise σ: the lines fan out and the CI widens. Raise n: SSTₓ grows and everything tightens.
    5. 
          
  5. Set β₁ = 0. The test now rejects in about α of the samples: the type I error rate.

    The regressors: x₂ against x₁
    
            
    
            
    The more the cloud collapses onto a line, the less independent variation x₁ has once x₂ is held fixed. That leftover variation is all OLS can use.
    
          
    
          
    
            
    Where se(β̂₁) comes from
    
            
    
            
    
            
    
            
    Var(β̂₁) = σ̂² / [SST₁ (1 − r₁²)] = (σ̂² / SST₁) × VIF. The first factor is the simple-regression variance; the VIF is the price of correlation.

    Try this
    
        
      
          
    1. With curvature on, the straight line leaves a U-shaped pattern in its residuals.
      2. 
          
    2. Move “Evaluate at x”. In the quadratic model the effect of x changes with x: β̂₁ + 2β̂₂x.
      3. 
          
    3. Set curvature to 0. The x² term turns insignificant and adjusted R² may fall when it is added.
      4. 
          
    4. Add irrelevant regressors. R² creeps up every time; adjusted R² does not.
      5. 
          
    5. Raise n. The CI for the mean prediction shrinks towards zero; the interval for one new observation never gets narrower than about ±2σ.
      6.

    Error variance
    
        
    
        γ = 0 is homoskedastic: Var(u | x) is constant. γ > 0: the spread of u grows with x₁. γ < 0: it shrinks.
        
    
        
    
        
    
        
    Try this
    
        
      
          
    1. Compare the two columns of the table. The coefficients are identical: heteroskedasticity does not bias OLS.
      2. 
          
    2. Run the repeated samples at γ = 1. The usual SE understates the true spread, so its 95% CIs cover less than 95%. Robust CIs are close to 95%.
      3. 
          
    3. Set γ = 0. Usual and robust SEs agree, and the Breusch–Pagan test rejects only about 5% of the time.
      4. 
          
    4. Try γ = −0.8. Now the usual SE is too large: inference is conservative rather than overconfident.
      5. 
          
    5. Drop n to 30. Robust SEs are a large-sample fix; in small samples they are imperfect too.
      6. 
        
    
        
    regress y x1 x2
estat hettest, rhs iid   // Breusch–Pagan, n·R²
estat imtest, white      // White test
regress y x1 x2, vce(robust)

Try this
  1. Your line starts flat at ȳ. Its SSR equals SST: this is predicting with the mean and ignoring x.
  2. Hide the OLS line and tilt yours to make SSR as small as you can. Then show OLS. You cannot beat it.
  3. Move the height away from ȳ. SSR grows by exactly n·d²: the best line always passes through (x̄, ȳ).
  4. Set σ = 0. Every point is on the line, r = ±1 and r² = 1.
  5. Now slide σ slowly from 0 up to 20 and watch the bars. The residual part SSR grows with the square of the noise and soon dominates SST, so r² = SSE/SST collapses. The explained part SSE changes far less: it depends on the slope, not on the noise. β̂₁ becomes less precise (press “Draw new sample” a few times at σ = 20 and watch it jump around 2), but it is not biased. A low R² means a lot of unexplained variation, not a wrong slope.
  6. Now go the other way: keep σ fixed and move the true slope from 0 up to 6. SSE grows with the square of the slope, because ŷᵢ − ȳ = β̂₁(xᵢ − x̄) gives SSE = β̂₁²·SSTₓ. SSR does not change at all, since the noise is the same. So r² rises: it measures the variation driven by x relative to the noise. The intercept plays no role, because every sum of squares is measured around ȳ. At slope 0, r and r² collapse towards 0.

OLS solves ∂SSR/∂β̂₀ = 0 and ∂SSR/∂β̂₁ = 0: the bottom of this parabola. Your SSR = SSR at your slope + n·d², where d is how far your line misses ȳ at x̄.

All four functional forms on the same data

          

          
R² in a log-y model measures how much of the variation in ln y is explained, so it cannot be compared with R² from a level-y model.

        

        

          
Comparing fit across y and ln y

          
Log-log and log-level share the dependent variable ln y, so their R² can be compared directly. To compare a log-y model with a level-y model, put both on the same scale: predict y in levels and measure fit there.

          

          
Converting log predictions back to levels

          

          

          
corr(y, ŷ)²: the upper bound for 1 − SSR/SST

          

          

            unadjusted: exp(ln ŷ)
            adjusted: smearing × exp(ln ŷ)
            45° line: perfect prediction
          

          

            
Why exp(ln ŷ) is too low. The model predicts the mean of ln y. Exponentiating gives exp(E[ln y]), which is smaller than E[y] (the exponential is convex). On average the unadjusted predictions sit below the 45° line.

            
Three adjustment factors scale them back up:

            
normal errors: exp(σ̂² / 2)  ·  smearing: mean of exp(ûᵢ)  ·  best in levels: α̌ = Σ yᵢ m̂ᵢ / Σ m̂ᵢ², where m̂ᵢ = exp(ln ŷᵢ)

            
The first assumes the errors are normal. Smearing does not. α̌ is the multiplier that minimises SSR in levels (regress y m̂, noconstant).

            
Two ways to compute an R² in levels. corr(y, ŷ)² is not affected by any adjustment factor, because multiplying ŷ by a constant does not change a correlation. It is the fit you would get after the best possible rescaling, an upper bound, and it is the number to compare with the level model's R². 1 − SSR/SST scores the predictions as they are: it punishes the unadjusted ones for being too low and can even be negative. For OLS fitted values of y itself (the level-level model) the two coincide.

            
Raise the noise to see the gap: with small σ the factors are all close to 1 and it barely matters.

          

          
regress lny x                 // or lnx for log-log
predict lnyhat, xb
gen uhat = lny - lnyhat
gen mhat = exp(lnyhat)        // unadjusted
corr y mhat
display r(rho)^2              // R² in levels, comparable with reg y x
gen eu = exp(uhat)
quietly summarize eu
gen yhat_s = r(mean)*mhat     // smearing-adjusted predictions
regress y mhat, noconstant    // coefficient = α̌

    Where se(β̂₁) comes from
    
            
    
            
    
            
    
            
    Var(β̂₁) = σ̂² / [SST₁ (1 − r₁²)] = (σ̂² / SST₁) × VIF. The first factor is the simple-regression variance; the VIF is the price of correlation.

            (β̂₁, β̂₂) at the chosen correlation

    Why repeat the experiment. The standard error is a prediction: how much β̂₁ would vary if we drew new data again and again. A single sample gives one β̂₁, which can land close to the truth even when its SE is huge. Here we redraw the data 500 times, at the chosen correlation and at zero correlation, and look at what the estimates actually do.
    
            
    The spread really is inflated. The SD of β̂₁ at this correlation divided by its SD at zero correlation is close to √VIF = 1/√(1 − corr²). The formula Var(β̂₁) = σ² / [SST₁ (1 − r₁²)] is describing something real.
    
            
    But there is no bias. The mean of β̂₁ stays near the true −1. Multicollinearity makes estimates noisy, not wrong on average. That is the difference from omitting a variable.
    
            
    The cloud is a diagonal. When x₁ and x₂ move together, a sample that overstates β₁ tends to understate β₂, and the reverse. The data pin down their combined effect well but not how to split it between the two. That is why the joint F-test can reject while neither t-test does, as the last count shows.

    t-test of H₀: β₁ = c
    
          
    
          
    
            Rejection region (total area α)
            observed t
          
    
          
    
          
    
            
    What the t-statistic measures. How many standard errors the estimate lies from the hypothesised value:
    
            
    t = (β̂₁ − c) / se(β̂₁)   ~   t with n − k − 1 df if H₀ is true
    
            
    If H₀ is true, β̂₁ is centred on c and t is usually small. A t beyond the critical value is so unlikely under H₀ (probability α) that we reject H₀. The p-value is the probability, under H₀, of a t at least this far from 0.
    
            
    Careful with Stata. The t and p columns in the regression output always test c = 0. For any other value, compute t yourself or use test x1 = c, which reports F = t².
    
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Confidence interval for β₁
    
          
    
          
    
          
    
            
    A CI is every c the test would not reject. Solving |β̂₁ − c| / se ≤ tcrit for c gives β̂₁ ± tcrit·se(β̂₁). So c lies inside the (1 − α) interval exactly when the two-sided test at level α fails to reject.
    
            
    What “95%” means. The procedure captures the true β₁ in 95% of samples. A single interval either contains β₁ or it does not; the repeated samples below show this.

    How the F-statistic works
    
          
    
            
    Dropping variables always raises SSR. Even irrelevant variables absorb a bit of the residuals by chance. The question is whether SSR rises by more than chance would produce:
    
            
    F = [(SSRR − SSRU) / q] / [SSRU / (n − k − 1)] = [(R²U − R²R) / q] / [(1 − R²U) / (n − k − 1)]
    
            
    The numerator is the extra unexplained variation per dropped variable (q = 2 here). The denominator is σ̂² from the unrestricted model, the benchmark for chance. It comes from the unrestricted model because that estimate of σ² is valid whether or not H₀ is true. Under H₀, F is close to 1 on average and follows an F(q, n − k − 1) distribution.
    
            
    Why not just two t-tests? Each t-test asks whether one variable matters holding the other fixed. When x₂ and x₃ move together there is little variation in one with the other held fixed, so both standard errors are large (high VIF) and both t's look small. The F-test asks whether the two matter together, and that the data can answer.

    Is the curvature real? Testing H₀: β₂ = 0
    
          
    
          
    
            
    A pattern is evidence, not proof. Residual plots are a good first look, but random noise also produces bumps. To decide whether the curvature is real, add x² and test its coefficient with an ordinary t-test:
    
            
    t = β̂₂ / se(β̂₂)   ~   t with n − 3 df if H₀: β₂ = 0 is true
    
            
    Rejecting H₀ means the straight line is the wrong functional form. Failing to reject does not prove the line is right: with a small sample or gentle curvature the test may simply miss it. That is a question of power, which the repeated samples below make visible.

The more curvature in the data, the further right (or left) the histogram moves and the more often the test detects it.     Marginal effect and prediction at the chosen x
    
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    R² vs adjusted R² as junk regressors pile up
    
          
    
          
    
            R²
            Adjusted R²
          
    
          
    Each step adds one pure-noise regressor to the quadratic model.
    
          
    
            
    Why R² only goes up. R² = 1 − SSR / SST. A new regressor can never raise SSR, because OLS could always give it a coefficient of zero. Even pure noise is correlated with the residuals by chance and absorbs a little of them, so SSR falls and R² rises.
    
            
    Why adjusted R² usually goes down. It replaces sums of squares with variances:
    
            
    R̄² = 1 − [SSR / (n − k − 1)] / [SST / (n − 1)] = 1 − σ̂² / s²y
    
            
    Adding a regressor lowers SSR a little but also lowers n − k − 1 by one. If SSR falls proportionally less than the degrees of freedom, σ̂² rises and R̄² falls. This happens exactly when the new variable has |t| < 1.
    
            
    What the chart shows. For a pure-noise regressor, |t| < 1 about two times in three, so adjusted R² drifts down. The occasional uptick is a junk variable that got |t| > 1 by luck. R² rewards every added variable; adjusted R² charges one degree of freedom for each.

    If a standard error is right, its bell curve should trace the histogram. A curve narrower than the histogram means that SE understates the real uncertainty, so its confidence intervals are too short and its tests reject too often.
    
          
    
          
    
          
    
            
    What goes wrong: false positives. If H₀ is true, a 5% test should reject about 5% of the time, and a 95% CI should contain the truth in about 95% of samples. Here the true β₁ is −1, so testing H₀: β₁ = −1 is a test of a true null. With the usual SE and γ > 0, it rejects far more than 5% of the time, and the “95%” CI covers the truth much less often. The two failures are the same thing: a CI misses the truth exactly when the test of the true value rejects. Robust SEs bring both back to roughly 5% and 95%.
    
            
    Caveat 1: the direction depends on the pattern. The usual SE is not always too small. What matters is where the noise sits. Observations far from the mean of x₁ carry the most information about the slope. If the error variance is largest there (the fan), the usual SE is too small. Switch the pattern to “spread largest in the middle of x₁”: now the noisy observations are the uninformative ones, the usual SE is too large, you under-reject, and coverage is above 95%. The same happens with a dummy regressor when the smaller group has the smaller variance. Inference is then conservative rather than overconfident, but still wrong.
    
            
    Caveat 2: this is about size, not power. Size is how often you reject a true null (false positives). Power is how often you detect a real effect. Heteroskedasticity distorts size, which is what the coverage bars measure here. Whether you can detect a true effect is a separate question.
